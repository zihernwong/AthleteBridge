Educate yo'self, fr

The stuff that lowkey controls your money — how the economy moves markets, and which accounts let you keep more of what you make. Explained no cap.

Term 1 of 4

GDP

The economy's report card, no cap
Gross Domestic Product is the total value of everything a country produces in a year — goods, services, all of it. Think of it as the economy's GPA. When GDP grows, businesses are printing, people have jobs, and the market usually follows. When GDP goes negative two quarters in a row, that's a recession era and portfolios get cooked. The 2020 COVID crash dropped GDP by nearly 3% — the worst since the Great Depression.
  • GDP rising → bull market energy
  • GDP negative → recession szn

Term 2 of 4

Inflation

Why your $20 hits different than it used to
Inflation is prices going up over time across the whole economy. A little bit (~2%) is actually healthy and what the Fed targets. But when inflation goes 8%+ like in 2022? Everything gets expensive — groceries, rent, gas — and the Fed has to step in with rate hikes that tank growth stocks. Moderate inflation is fine for equities. Runaway inflation is the villain arc.
  • ~2% inflation → market slays
  • 3–5% → market gets nervous
  • 8%+ → bear market szn

Term 3 of 4

CPI

The receipts on how expensive life is getting
Consumer Price Index tracks the price of a fixed basket of everyday items — groceries, rent, gas, clothes. It's literally how the government measures inflation. Every month when CPI data drops, the whole market reacts in real time. CPI coming in hotter than expected = red day. CPI cooling down = green day. Wall Street watches this number like it's a Kendrick album drop.
  • CPI cooling → rally incoming
  • CPI hot → market dips on the news

Interest Rates

The Fed's main character energy that moves everything
The Federal Reserve sets the benchmark interest rate to control inflation. When rates go up, borrowing gets expensive — mortgages, car loans, business credit all cost more. Tech and growth stocks especially hate high rates because their future profits get discounted hard. The Fed went from 0.25% → 5.5% in under 2 years (2022–2023) — one of the fastest hike cycles ever, and the market felt it fr.
  • Rate cuts → market goes brr
  • Rate hikes → growth stocks cooked

How it all connects

These four terms aren't separate — they're one big chain reaction. Here's how it plays out.

▲ Bull scenario
  1. GDP grows strong — economy is healthy, companies printing money
  2. Inflation stays near 2% — prices rising but not crazy
  3. Fed holds or cuts rates — borrowing stays cheap, business invests
  4. CPI stays chill — consumers keep spending, confidence high
  5. Stocks go up. Portfolio secured.
▼ Bear scenario
  1. Inflation spikes — supply chain issues, too much money printed
  2. CPI data comes in hot — everyday prices going up fast
  3. Fed hikes interest rates aggressively to cool inflation down
  4. GDP starts slowing — businesses borrow less, hiring freezes
  5. Stocks drop. Market in its villain era.

Same investments, different tax bill

An account is just a wrapper around your investments. You can hold the exact same S&P 500 fund in every one of these — what changes is when the IRS takes its cut. There are three moments it can: when money goes in, while it grows, and when it comes out. A regular brokerage account gets taxed along the way. The others are "tax-advantaged": each one skips at least one of those moments, in exchange for rules about limits and withdrawals.

The flexible one

Taxable brokerage

No rules, no perks, taxes as you go
This is the plain account you open on Robinhood, Fidelity or Schwab. You put in money you've already paid income tax on, and anyone can open one. Dividends and interest get taxed the year you receive them. When you sell for a profit you owe capital gains tax: hold for more than a year and it's the long-term rate (0%, 15% or 20% depending on income); sell sooner and it's taxed like your paycheck. Sell at a loss and you can use it to cancel out gains.
  • No limits, no income rules, take money out whenever
  • The only one here with no tax break at all
The work one

Traditional 401(k)

Tax break now, tax bill later
Offered through your job. Contributions come out of your paycheck before income tax, so putting in $5,000 lowers your taxable pay by $5,000 this year. It grows untaxed, then every dollar you take out in retirement is taxed as regular income. Many employers add a match — free money on top of what you put in. Take money out before 59½ and you usually owe a 10% penalty plus the tax. From age 73 the IRS makes you start withdrawing.
  • Employer match is the closest thing to free money
  • Highest limit of the bunch: $24,500 in 2026
  • Locked up until 59½, and withdrawals are fully taxed
The work one, flipped

Roth 401(k)

Pay the tax now, never again
Same workplace plan, opposite tax deal. You contribute money that's already been taxed, so there's no break today — but the growth and the withdrawals in retirement are completely tax-free. It shares the $24,500 limit with the traditional 401(k), so you can split between them but not double up. Unlike a Roth IRA there's no income cap, so high earners can use it. Not every employer offers one.
  • Decades of growth come out tax-free
  • No income limit
  • No tax break this year, and still locked until 59½
The do-it-yourself one

Traditional IRA

A 401(k)-style deal you open yourself
An Individual Retirement Account isn't tied to a job — you open it at any brokerage, as long as you have earned income. Contributions are usually tax-deductible, growth isn't taxed, and withdrawals are taxed as income. The catch: if you also have a retirement plan at work, the deduction fades out as your income rises (between $81,000 and $91,000 for a single filer in 2026). Same 10% early-withdrawal penalty before 59½, same forced withdrawals from 73.
  • Works even if your job has no retirement plan
  • Low limit: $7,500, shared with the Roth IRA
  • Deduction can shrink or vanish if you have a plan at work
The fan favorite

Roth IRA

Tax-free forever, if you get in while you can
You put in already-taxed money, and everything it earns comes out tax-free in retirement. It's the most flexible retirement account: you can take back what you contributed (not the earnings) at any time with no tax or penalty, and you're never forced to withdraw. The trade-off is an income cap — in 2026 the amount you can contribute shrinks between $153,000 and $168,000 of income for single filers ($242,000–$252,000 for married couples), then hits zero.
  • Tax-free growth, and no forced withdrawals ever
  • Your contributions stay reachable in an emergency
  • Income limits, and only $7,500 a year
The sleeper

HSA

The only account that skips tax all three times
A Health Savings Account is meant for medical bills, but you can invest the balance like any other account. Money goes in untaxed, grows untaxed, and comes out untaxed when spent on qualified medical costs — no other account does all three. You can only contribute while you're on a high-deductible health plan. Spend it on non-medical stuff before 65 and you owe income tax plus a 20% penalty; after 65 the penalty goes away and it works like a traditional IRA.
  • Triple tax break, and the money rolls over every year
  • Only available with a high-deductible health plan
  • Steep 20% penalty for non-medical spending before 65
The school one

529 plan

Tax-free growth for tuition
A state-run investment account for education. You contribute already-taxed money (some states give you a deduction for it), it grows untaxed, and withdrawals are tax-free when spent on education — tuition, housing, books. Use it on something else and the earnings get taxed plus a 10% penalty. There's no yearly federal cap, though big gifts can run into gift-tax rules above $19,000 per person. If the money isn't needed, up to $35,000 over a lifetime can move into the student's Roth IRA once the account is 15 years old.
  • Tax-free growth, and anyone can contribute for a kid
  • Earnings are penalized if not used for education
The big question

Traditional or Roth?

It comes down to when your tax rate is lower
Both end up taxing your money once — the question is when. Traditional gives you the break today and taxes you in retirement. Roth taxes you today and never again. If you expect to be in a higher bracket later (early in your career, income still climbing), paying now with a Roth tends to win. If you're in a high bracket now and expect a lower one in retirement, the traditional deduction is worth more. Nobody knows future tax rates, which is why plenty of people split between the two.
  • Lower income now → Roth usually looks better
  • Higher income now → traditional usually looks better
  • Not sure → some of each covers both outcomes

A common order to fill them in

A rule of thumb a lot of people follow once they have an emergency fund and no high-interest debt. It's a starting point, not personal advice — your job's plan, income and goals change the answer.

  1. 401(k) up to the employer match — skipping the match is turning down part of your pay
  2. HSA, if you have a high-deductible health plan — the only triple tax break
  3. Roth or traditional IRA — more investment choices than most work plans
  4. The rest of your 401(k) — back to the big limit
  5. Taxable brokerage — for everything beyond the limits, or money you need before retirement

Limits and income ranges are the IRS figures for the 2026 tax year and change most years. This covers US accounts and federal tax only; states have their own rules.
